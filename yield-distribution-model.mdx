#### **The Yield Distribution Model**

Yield distribution between tranches works similarly to lending markets. In Morpho or Aave, high utilization pushes supply rates up to attract lenders, and vice versa. Royco utilizes a similar mechanism, with a different definition of utilization. This model is applied to both the JT risk premium for providing coverage, and the LT liquidity premium.

#### **Coverage utilization → Junior's risk premium**

**In Royco, coverage utilization measures how much JT capital is currently backing ST exposure.** For example, if the market needs \$1 of Junior coverage and there is exactly \$1 of Junior, utilization is 100%. If there is \$2 of Junior, it is 50%. When JT capital is scarce, more yield flows to JT to attract more first-loss capital. When JT capital is abundant, more yield flows to ST. The pool rebalances toward equilibrium based on where each side wants to deploy.

The protocol targets **90% utilization** as the equilibrium point:

- **Senior depositors** need the 10% buffer. At 100%, the market closes to new Senior deposits because there’s no Junior left to back them.
- **Junior depositors** need utilization high enough that their boosted yield is meaningful. At low utilization, Junior’s premium gets diluted across the pool and the risk isn’t sufficiently compensated. At 90%, Junior earns real extra yield, and the curve steepens aggressively above that point; so when coverage gets tight, new Junior capital is strongly incentivized to show up.

#### **Liquidity utilization → the Liquidity Tranche's liquidity premium**

The same model is also applied to provide secondary liquidity instead of coverage. Here, utilization measures how much of the market's required secondary liquidity the LT is currently providing. When liquidity is scarce, more yield flows to the LT through the liquidity premium in order to attract more market-making capital. When liquidity is abundant, more yield stays with the ST. The mechanics are identical to coverage; only the metric has changed.

The same **90% target** applies, for the same reasons:

- **Senior depositors** need secondary liquidity above the market's minimum so their shares remain tradable. At 100%, the LT is exactly at its floor, and the market stops accepting new ST deposits because any additional ST would outrun the available liquidity.
- **LT depositors** need utilization high enough that the liquidity premium is meaningful. At low utilization the premium gets diluted and the capital commitment isn't sufficiently compensated. At 90%, the LT earns real extra yield, and the curve steepens aggressively above that point; so when secondary liquidity gets tight, new market-making capital is strongly incentivized to show up.

#### **One source of yield, capped by ST yield.**

Both premiums are paid out of ST yield. Each is capped per market, set somewhere between 0% and 100% of Senior yield. The two caps are constrained together so that the **risk premium plus the liquidity premium never exceeds 100% of ST yield**. ST gives up part of its yield to JT for coverage and part to LT for liquidity, and keeps the rest.
